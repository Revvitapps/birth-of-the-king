How to Use This Book

Welcome to Beyond the Manger: A Detailed Look into the Story of the Promised King, an all-in-one curriculum that walks your family through the story of Yeshua's birth, from the promises of the prophets to the family's journey home to Nazareth. Everything you need to teach is in this one book: the reading, your Teaching Nuggets, and the student activity pages, placed right where they belong in each lesson.

How Each Lesson Works

Lesson Plan. Every lesson opens with a one-page plan: the Bible passages, the lesson goals, an opening prayer, the big picture, and the order of the readings and activities.

Reading Spreads. Each topic is a two-page spread, with an illustration on the left and the reading on the right. Read the right-hand page aloud while everyone looks at the picture.

Teaching Nuggets. The gold-edged column beside the reading holds background, word studies, and talking points for you. Think of these as bite-sized, digestible key takeaways and extra hidden gems inside the lesson.

Did You Know? The gold boxes hold a fun or surprising fact to share.

Activity Pages. Coloring pages and worksheets follow the reading they belong to. Answers are in the Answer Key at the back of the book.

Printable Student Pages

Every coloring page and worksheet in this book is also in a separate printable pack for each lesson, sized for standard 8.5 × 11 paper. Print as many copies as you need for your family.

Ages and Pacing

This book is for people of all ages. Whether young or old, there is something new you are bound to discover. You can go at your own pace, according to what fits best for your family. Don't feel pressed to walk through all the information at one time. Use this book year after year to come back and uncover something new in the amazing story of the Messiah.

A Note on Names and Sources

Yeshua and Jesus

In this book you will see both names, Yeshua and Jesus. Yeshua is the Hebrew name that Mary and Joseph gave their son. It means “God saves” or “God is salvation.” Jesus is the same name after it passed through Greek and Latin into English. Using His Hebrew name helps children remember that Jesus was born into a Hebrew family, in the land of Israel, as the promised King from David's line.

Bible Verses

The verses printed at the top of each reading are from the King James Version. Feel free to read the passages aloud from the Bible translation your class uses.

History and Tradition

Some parts of this study, such as the Tower of the Flock, the season of Yeshua's birth, and the homeland of the Wise Men, draw on Jewish history, ancient writings, and the work of Bible scholars. Where good teachers disagree, the Teaching Nuggets show the different views. The Bible itself is always the final word.

Lesson One · Reading 1

The Promised King: Son of David

1
“Behold, the days come, saith the LORD, that I will raise unto David a righteous Branch, and a King shall reign and prosper” (Jeremiah 23:5)
Read together: Matthew 1:1 · 2 Samuel 7:12–16 · Isaiah 11:1–2

Long ago, God made a promise to King David, the shepherd boy from Bethlehem who became Israel’s greatest king. God told David that one of his descendants would sit on his throne, and that this King’s kingdom would last forever (2 Samuel 7:12–16).

For hundreds of years, God’s people waited. The prophets kept the promise alive. Jeremiah said God would raise up a “righteous Branch” for David. Isaiah said a shoot would grow from the stump of Jesse, David’s father, and the Spirit of the Lord would rest on Him (Isaiah 11:1–2).

Waiting for a King

By the time Yeshua was born, Israel was ruled by Rome. Many people were looking for a warrior king who would drive out the Romans with an army. The Pharisees, who carefully kept the Torah, believed they were protecting God’s ways. But God’s King came in a way almost no one expected: as a baby, born to a humble family.

“Son of David”

Matthew begins his Gospel with these words: “Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham.” Calling Yeshua the Son of David was a bold announcement. It was a title for the Messiah, the promised King. Matthew wanted every reader to know that Yeshua was the One God had promised.

Teaching Nuggets

Ask the class

What kind of king do you think people were hoping for? How is Yeshua different?

Fulfilled prophecy

Yeshua fulfilled more than 300 Old Testament prophecies. Have children find Jeremiah 23:5–6 and Isaiah 11:1–2 in their Bibles.

Key word

Messiah (Hebrew) and Christ (Greek) both mean “Anointed One,” the chosen King.

Notes
Did You Know?
Jesse, David’s father, lived in Bethlehem. That is why Isaiah calls the coming King a shoot from “the stem of Jesse.”

Two Family Lines: The Right to Be King

1
“And there shall come forth a rod out of the stem of Jesse, and a Branch shall grow out of his roots” (Isaiah 11:1)
Read together: Matthew 1:1–17 · Luke 3:23–38

The Bible gives two family trees for Yeshua. Matthew 1 and Luke 3 list His ancestors, but the lists are different. Why? Together they show that Yeshua had the right to be King through both sides of His family.

The Legal Right: Joseph’s Line

Matthew traces the family from Abraham to King David, and then through David’s son Solomon, the line of the kings, all the way to Joseph. Joseph was not Yeshua’s birth father, but by adopting Him, Joseph gave Yeshua the legal right to David’s throne.

The Blood Right: Mary’s Line

Luke traces the family through David’s son Nathan. Many Bible teachers believe this is Mary’s family line. Through Mary, Yeshua was truly born from David’s family. He had the blood right to the throne, just as Isaiah promised.

Adopted Into His Family

Here is good news: just as Yeshua was adopted into Joseph’s family, God adopts all who believe into His family. “God sent forth his Son … that we might receive the adoption of sons” (Galatians 4:4–5). Those who were far away are brought near “by the blood of Christ” (Ephesians 2:13).

Three Sets of Fourteen

Matthew arranges the names into three groups of fourteen generations (Matthew 1:17). Matthew even lists some women with surprising stories, like Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and Bathsheba, showing that God’s grace reaches everyone.

Teaching Nuggets

Legal and Blood

Legal right: Joseph, Solomon’s line (Jer. 23:5–6).

Blood right: Mary, Nathan’s line (Isa. 11).

Numbers

“14” is often linked to deliverance and salvation; “3” to divine completeness. Three sets of fourteen cover Israel’s long seasons of hardship before the Deliverer came.

Gospel link

Believers are adopted into God’s family (Gal. 4:4–5) and brought near by His blood (Eph. 2:13).

Notes
Did You Know?
Luke’s list goes all the way back to Adam, “which was the son of God” (Luke 3:38). There were about 2,000 years from Abraham to Yeshua.

An Angel Visits Mary

2
“Behold, a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel” (Isaiah 7:14)
Read together: Luke 1:26–38 · Matthew 1:18–25

In the hill country of Galilee sat a little village called Nazareth. In this quiet place lived a young woman named Mary, who was promised in marriage to a carpenter named Joseph.

One day God sent the angel Gabriel to Mary. “Greetings, you who are highly favored! The Lord is with you,” he said. Mary was troubled, but Gabriel told her not to be afraid. She was going to give birth to a son, who would be called the Son of the Highest, and she was to call Him Yeshua. “The Lord God shall give unto him the throne of his father David,” Gabriel said, “and of his kingdom there shall be no end” (Luke 1:32–33).

“How can this be?” Mary asked. Gabriel explained that the Holy Spirit would come upon her, so the child would be called the Son of God. “For with God nothing shall be impossible.” Mary answered with a trusting heart: “Behold the handmaid of the Lord; be it unto me according to thy word.”

Joseph’s Dream

When Joseph learned that Mary was expecting a baby, he was worried and did not know what to do. Then an angel spoke to him in a dream: the baby was from the Holy Spirit, and He would save His people from their sins. Joseph woke up, obeyed God, and took Mary home as his wife.

Teaching Nuggets

Before you begin

Open in prayer, asking God to prepare the children’s hearts to be open and ready to receive understanding of today’s lesson.

“Son of David”

Gabriel’s words point straight back to God’s promise to David. Ask: which promise from Reading 1 is coming true?

Mary’s faith

Mary did not understand everything, but she trusted God. Ask: When is it hard to trust God?

Notes

This would not be the only time God would speak to Joseph through a dream! God spoke to him in four dreams in all (Matthew 1:20; 2:13; 2:19–20; 2:22).
The Hebrew word for salvation is ישועה (yeshu’ah), which means deliverance or rescue. It comes from the root ישע (Y-Sh-A), meaning to save or rescue. Listen closely: it sounds just like His name, Yeshua!

His Name Is Yeshua

2
“And she shall bring forth a son, and thou shalt call his name JESUS: for he shall save his people from their sins” (Matthew 1:21)
Read together: Matthew 1:20–25 · Isaiah 49:6

In Bible times, a name was more than a label. It often told something about who a person was or what God would do through them. So when the angel told Joseph what to name the baby, the name carried a message.

Mary and Joseph did not choose this name. Heaven chose it! In Hebrew, the name is Yeshua. This is a shorter form of Yehoshua (Joshua). It is made up from two Hebrew words: “Yah,” a shortened form of Yahweh, the personal name of God, and the verb “Yasha,” meaning to deliver, save or rescue. Put together, the meaning is “God is Salvation.” The angel even explained it: “for he shall save his people from their sins.”

Yah (יָה): a shortened form of Yahweh, the personal name of God.

Yasha (יָשַׁע): a verb meaning to deliver, save, or rescue.

From Yeshua to Jesus

How did Yeshua become “Jesus”? The New Testament was written in Greek. Greek has no “sh” sound, and Greek names for men end in “s,” so Yeshua was written Iēsous. Latin kept it as Iesus. The first King James Bible, printed in 1611, still spelled it “Iesus.” The letter J came later. The spelling changed. The Person and the meaning never did.

Immanuel: God With Us

Matthew explains that all this happened to fulfill Isaiah’s prophecy: the virgin would have a son, and they would call Him Immanuel, which means “God with us” (Matthew 1:23). Yeshua is both. He is the God who saves, and the God who came to be with His people.

Teaching Nuggets

Name study

Yeshua is the same name as Joshua in the Old Testament. Joshua led Israel into the Promised Land; Yeshua leads His people into God’s kingdom.

Joseph’s obedience

Joseph “did as the angel of the Lord had bidden him” (Matt. 1:24). Ask: How did Joseph show faith?

Activity tip

Hebrew is written and read from the right side of the page to the left. Show the class before they try the writing activity.

Notes
Did You Know?
The name Yeshua appears in Hebrew as ישוע. It is made from four Hebrew letters: Yod, Shin, Vav, and Ayin.

Betrothed: A Promise Before the Wedding

2
“When as his mother Mary was espoused to Joseph, before they came together, she was found with child of the Holy Ghost” (Matthew 1:18)
Read together: Matthew 1:18–25 · Genesis 29:27 · John 14:2–3 · Revelation 19:7

Matthew says Mary was “espoused” to Joseph. That is betrothal, and in Israel a betrothal was far more than an engagement. To understand what Joseph faced, it helps to see how the Bible describes a marriage being made.

A covenant came first. The two families agreed together, and the groom’s side gave a bride price, or “dowry” (Genesis 34:12; Exodus 22:16–17). From that day the couple belonged to each other by covenant. A covenant is a binding and unbreakable commitment. Scripture already calls a betrothed woman a “wife” (Deuteronomy 22:23–24). That is why Matthew calls Joseph “her husband” before the wedding, and why ending a betrothal took a divorce (Matthew 1:19).

Then came the waiting. The bride stayed in her father’s house and made herself ready. The groom went home to prepare a place for her. Neither knew the exact day the waiting would end.

When all was ready, the groom came for his bride with his friends, often at night, and the bride’s companions met him with burning lamps (Matthew 25:1–10). He brought her to his home, and the families feasted for a week (Genesis 29:27; Judges 14:12).

Mary and Joseph were in the waiting period. The covenant was made, but Joseph had not yet brought Mary home. Then Mary was found to be with child. Joseph was a just man, and he was also a kind one. He planned to end the betrothal quietly. God stopped him with a dream, and Joseph “took unto him his wife” (Matthew 1:24).

Why it matters. Yeshua used this same picture for Himself. “I go to prepare a place for you. And if I go and prepare a place for you, I will come again, and receive you unto myself” (John 14:2–3). He is the Bridegroom. His people are in the waiting, making themselves ready. The story ends with a feast: “the marriage of the Lamb is come, and his wife hath made herself ready” (Revelation 19:7).

Teaching Nuggets

Key words

Later Jewish teachers named the two steps erusin (the betrothal) and nisuin (the bringing home). The written marriage contract is called a ketubah.

Picture and fulfillment

Covenant made → the cross.

The waiting → today.

The groom returns → John 14:3.

The feast → Revelation 19:7–9.

Discuss

Joseph obeyed before he understood everything. What made that hard? What made it right?

Notes
Did You Know?
Yeshua’s first miracle took place at a wedding feast in Cana, only a few miles from Nazareth (John 2:1–11).

The Census

3
“And it came to pass in those days, that there went out a decree from Caesar Augustus, that all the world should be taxed” (Luke 2:1)
Read together: Luke 2:1–5 · Micah 5:2

Far away in the city of Rome, the emperor Caesar Augustus made a law. Everyone in the Roman Empire had to be counted in a census and registered for taxes. Each family had to go to its own hometown to sign up.

Joseph belonged to the family of King David, and David’s hometown was Bethlehem in Judea. So Joseph had to leave Nazareth and travel south to Bethlehem, and he took Mary with him, even though her baby would soon be born.

A Long Journey

The trip from Nazareth to Bethlehem was about 90 miles. Traveling on foot, perhaps with a donkey to carry supplies, it would have taken about a week. Many travelers followed the road along the Jordan River valley and then climbed up through the hills past Jerusalem.

God Was in Charge

Caesar Augustus thought he was in control of the whole world. But God was using even an emperor’s law to keep His promise! Seven hundred years earlier, the prophet Micah had written that the Ruler of Israel would be born in Bethlehem (Micah 5:2). The census brought Mary and Joseph to exactly the right town at exactly the right time.

So with the baby’s birth only weeks away, Joseph and Mary packed what they could carry and started south.

Teaching Nuggets

God’s timing

Galatians 4:4 says God sent His Son “when the fulness of the time was come.” Ask: How did God use the census?

Rulers

Caesar Augustus ruled Rome from 27 BC to AD 14. Quirinius governed Syria (Luke 2:2).

Map it

Trace the route from Nazareth to Bethlehem on a map of Israel.

Notes
Did You Know?

Migdal Eder: The Tower of the Flock

3
“And thou, O tower of the flock, the strong hold of the daughter of Zion, unto thee shall it come, even the first dominion” (Micah 4:8)
Read together: Genesis 35:19–21 · Micah 4:8 · Micah 5:2 · Luke 2:8–12

Right at the edge of Bethlehem, in the fields just outside the town, stood a stone watchtower called Migdal Eder. In Hebrew, Migdal Eder means “Tower of the Flock.” It was so close to Bethlehem that the shepherds who worked there could walk into town in a few minutes.

Where Rachel Was Buried

The Tower of the Flock first appears in the story of Jacob. When Jacob’s wife Rachel died, she “was buried in the way to Ephrath, which is Bethlehem,” and Jacob set up a pillar on her grave (Genesis 35:19–20). The very next verse says Jacob “spread his tent beyond the tower of Edar” (Genesis 35:21). Edar is the same Hebrew word as Eder. So from Israel’s earliest days, the Tower of the Flock stood right beside Bethlehem.

A Prophecy the Shepherds Knew

Hundreds of years later, the prophet Micah spoke to the Tower of the Flock: “Unto thee shall it come, even the first dominion” (Micah 4:8). A few verses later he announced that the Ruler of Israel would come from Bethlehem (Micah 5:2). The shepherds of Migdal Eder knew these prophecies about their own tower. Clues for a Fall Birth

Shepherds in the fields. In Judea, shepherds stayed out in the fields with their flocks at night from spring until early fall. The weather was mild enough to sleep outdoors.

The feasts. The fall feasts, Trumpets, the Day of Atonement, and Tabernacles (Sukkot), brought crowds of travelers. That would explain why there was no room! A Roman census would also make sense in a season when people were already traveling.

Clues for a Spring Birth

Some Bible scholars count Zechariah’s service differently, placing John’s birth in March and Yeshua’s around March or April.

Spring is lambing season, when shepherds kept watch day and night.

Some early Christian writers placed the birth in March or April.

What Matters Most

Both views are possible, but calculations that use the Hebrew calendar point most strongly to the fall. What matters most is not the date, but that God kept His promise right on time.

Hebrew calendar

The Hebrew calendar follows the moon and adds leap months. The feasts fall on fixed Hebrew dates. Using the modern calendar can shift the calculations.

Why was it built? God wanted to live among His people (Exodus 25:8). King David dreamed of building a house for God, and God gave that job to David’s son, King Solomon. The temple was the one place where all Israel came to worship the one true God.

The temple Yeshua knew. Solomon’s temple was later destroyed, and God’s people built it again (Ezra 6:15). In the days of King Herod it was made bigger and more beautiful. That work went on for forty-six years (John 2:20)!

5

Read together: Matthew 2:1–2 · Daniel 2:48 · Numbers 24:17

Many songs call them “three kings,” but the Bible never says they were kings. Matthew calls them magoi, or Magi, a well-known group of priest-scholars from the lands east of Israel.

Priests, Scholars, and King-Makers

The Magi first came from the Medes and Persians. They were experts in the stars, astrologers, and students of dreams, ancient writings, and the business of kings. At the time Yeshua was born, Persia was part of the Parthian Empire, the great rival of Rome. In Parthia, Magi sat on the king’s council. Together with the nobles, they had so much power that they could help choose a new king, or remove one. People called them king-makers.

Why Would They Care About a Jewish King?

Hundreds of years earlier, the Jews were carried away to Babylon. One of them, Daniel, became “chief of the governors over all the wise men of Babylon” (Daniel 2:48). Daniel’s writings included a timetable for the coming Messiah (Daniel 9:24–27).

Many Jewish families stayed in Babylon and Persia for centuries. They knew the prophecy of Balaam: “There shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel” (Numbers 24:17). For scholars who studied the sky, a prophecy about a star would catch their attention! Generations of Eastern scholars may have kept and passed on this knowledge.

Teaching Nuggets

Key word

Magi (Greek magoi) is where the word “magic” comes from, but these men were not tricksters. They were respected priests and scholars.

Astronomy is studying the sun, moon, stars, and planets as God made them. Astrology is the belief that the stars control or foretell people’s lives, which the Bible warns against (Deuteronomy 18:10–12). The Magi practiced both, yet God used the sky they studied to lead them to Yeshua.

Daniel’s legacy

Daniel’s influence is a big reason many scholars believe Eastern Magi were expecting a Jewish King.

What was the star the Magi saw? Matthew does not explain, but he does give some clues.

What It Probably Was Not

It was probably not a comet, because no recorded comet matches the time. And it was not an ordinary star sitting still above one house. Matthew says the star “went before them” and then “stood over where the young child was.” That sounds like God’s own guidance. Many believe the Magi first noticed an important sign in the sky, and then God guided them by a special light, like the glory cloud that led Israel in the wilderness, for the last part of their journey.

How Many Wise Men Were There?

The Bible never says there were three. The number three comes from the three gifts: gold, frankincense, and myrrh. Important visitors from Parthia would never travel in a group of three! A royal delegation traveled with a large caravan of servants, guards, camels, and horses, possibly 80 to 100 people or more.

When Did They Come?

The Magi did not visit the manger on the night Yeshua was born. Matthew calls Him a young child (Greek paidion), not a newborn baby. The Magi found Him in a house (Matthew 2:11). And Herod later ordered the death of boys “two years old and under,” based on when the star first appeared (Matthew 2:16). The Magi most likely arrived when Yeshua was between six months and two years old.

Parthia: The Empire Rome Could Not Beat

5
“When Herod the king had heard these things, he was troubled, and all Jerusalem with him” (Matthew 2:3)
Read together: Matthew 2:1–3

Two Big Kingdoms, One River. Long ago, the mighty Roman Empire ruled all the lands around the Mediterranean Sea. But Rome’s power ended at a big river called the Euphrates. On the other side of the river was another powerful kingdom: the Parthian Empire. It covered the ancient lands of Babylon and Persia, the very same place where Daniel from the Bible had lived and worked. That river was like a giant border dividing two different worlds.

The Neighbors Rome Feared. The Romans were tough fighters, but they were actually afraid of the Parthians. Whenever Roman soldiers crossed that river to attack, they were beaten and sent running back. In fact, at a famous battle, fast-riding Parthian horsemen defeated an entire Roman army. Rome never forgot how strong their neighbors were.

Herod remembered too. King Herod never forgot it either! Years before Yeshua was born, the Parthian army marched right into Jerusalem, kicked Herod out, and put someone else on the throne. Herod had to run away to Rome to save his life. The Roman leaders officially named him the new king, and it took him three whole years, and a giant Roman army, to fight his way back and reclaim his throne.

The Magi’s Journey. The Magi likely set out from the Parthian capital, Seleucia–Ctesiphon, on the Tigris River. They would have followed the trade routes along the Euphrates River, turned south through Damascus, and come down to Jerusalem. The trip was about 700 to 900 miles and could take two to four months.

Now read Matthew 2:3 again. About thirty-five years after Herod lost his throne, a big group of important travelers rode into his city from that exact same eastern empire! They weren’t soldiers, though. They were the Magi, the special wise men whose job was to help pick the new kings of Parthia. And what were they asking? “Where is he that is born King of the Jews?”

No wonder Herod was terrified! He had lost his crown to people from the East once before, and now the king-makers from that very same land were in his city looking for a new king.

Herod saw a threat. The Magi saw a King. The same news about Yeshua still divides people the same way.

Two Kings in One Land

5

When the Wise Men arrived in Jerusalem asking for the baby “born King of the Jews,” there was already a man sitting on the throne who called himself that very same name.

To understand why this caused such big trouble, you have to know the difference between a king who is picked by people and a King who is born with God’s promise. Let’s put these two kings side by side!

1. Different Family Trees

King Herod: He wasn’t even from King David’s family! His family came from a neighboring land called Edom, which meant they were descendants of Esau (Jacob’s brother).

Yeshua (Jesus): God had promised that the true Savior-King would come from Jacob’s family, specifically through the tribe of Judah and the royal family of King David. Yeshua had the true royal bloodline.

2. A Crown Given vs. A Crown Born With

King Herod: He was made a king by powerful politicians. The Roman government handed him a crown and gave him the job. Because people gave him his power, he was always worried people could take it away.

Yeshua: He was born a King. Nobody voted for Him or handed Him a title. His right to rule came straight from God’s promise and His family line (Matthew 1:1; 2:2).

3. Building for Show vs. Building Forever

King Herod: He built massive, shiny things to show off how great he was, like a giant harbor city (Caesarea), a mountain fortress (Masada), a grand palace, and a stunning temple. He wanted everyone to be impressed by him, yet everything he built eventually crumbled.

Yeshua: He didn’t come to build stone palaces for Himself; He came to build God’s forever kingdom in people’s hearts.

4. Ruling with Fear vs. Ruling with Love

King Herod: Because Herod had to fight and scheme to get his throne, he lived in constant fear of losing it. He didn’t trust anyone, not even his own family, and had people put to death if he thought they might take his crown. To Herod, a baby in Bethlehem was just another threat to get rid of.

Yeshua: A true King doesn’t need to be afraid of babies or rivals. Yeshua came to rescue His people and rule with peace, justice, and love.

5. A King Who Died vs. A King Forever

King Herod: Herod’s time as king came to an end when he died, and his kingdom was split up among his sons (Matthew 2:22).

Yeshua: His rule will never stop. Just like the angel promised before He was born, “of his kingdom there shall be no end” (Luke 1:33).

Did You Know?
Herod was buried at Herodium, the fortress-palace he built near Bethlehem. Archaeologists found what is believed to be his tomb in 2007.

The Gifts: Gold, Frankincense, and Myrrh

6
“And when they had opened their treasures, they presented unto him gifts; gold, and frankincense, and myrrh” (Matthew 2:11)
Read together: Matthew 2:11 · Exodus 30:34–38 · Hebrews 4:14–16

When you look deeply into the gifts mentioned in Matthew 2, you quickly realize they were not simply offerings of honor, but a prophetic proclamation. Gold. Frankincense. Myrrh.

Matthew records that the Magi fell down and worshiped Him, and then opened their treasures and presented their gifts to Him. Each gift announced something essential about who Yeshua is and what His first coming would accomplish.

Gold: The King

Gold has always spoken the language of royalty. Throughout Scripture it is linked with kingship, authority, and enduring worth (1 Kings 10:18–25; Psalm 21:3). By offering gold, the Magi proclaimed that Yeshua was born a King. Not one who would seize power later, but one who held authority from the very beginning. Even before Yeshua preached a sermon or performed a miracle, the gold declared what Pilate would later question and the cross would surprisingly affirm: “Jesus of Nazareth the King of the Jews” (John 19:19).

Frankincense: The High Priest

Frankincense belonged to worship. In Israel it was reserved for sacred use, burned on the altar and mixed into the holy incense as an offering pleasing to the Lord (Exodus 30:34–38; Leviticus 2:1–2). The psalmist describes prayer rising to God “as incense” (Psalm 141:2), and Revelation links incense with the prayers of the saints before God’s throne (Revelation 5:8).

By offering frankincense, the Magi acknowledged that Yeshua was not only royal but holy, the One through whom true worship would be made possible (John 4:21–24). Frankincense quietly announces that He would be the High Priest, the One who stands between God and people (Hebrews 4:14–16).

Practical value

Lesson Six · Reading 3

Under an Israelite Roof

6
“And when they were come into the house, they saw the young child with Mary his mother” (Matthew 2:11)
Read together: Matthew 2:11 · Deuteronomy 22:8 · Mark 2:1–4 · Luke 15:8

Notice one word in Matthew 2:11: house. The shepherds found a baby in a manger. The Wise Men found a young child in a house. Time had passed, and the family had settled in Bethlehem. Scripture never describes that house, but the Bible is full of clues about what an ordinary home in Israel was like.

1. One lamp lit the whole house. Yeshua said a lamp on a stand “giveth light unto all that are in the house” (Matthew 5:15). Most homes had one main room. A whole family slept there together (Luke 11:7). The room was dim enough that a woman who lost a coin had to light a lamp and sweep to find it (Luke 15:8).

2. The roof was a room. Roofs were flat, reached by outside steps. God’s law required a low wall around the edge so no one would fall (Deuteronomy 22:8). Peter went up on a roof to pray (Acts 10:9). The roof itself was made of beams, branches and packed earth, which is how four friends could dig through one to lower a man down to Yeshua (Mark 2:4).

3. Guests had an upper room. Some homes had a small room built on top. A family in Shunem built one for the prophet Elisha, with a bed, a table, a stool and a lamp (2 Kings 4:10). This is the kind of “guest room” that was already full on the night Yeshua was born.

4. The animals were close. A family’s donkey or ox was kept in a stall and led out to water each morning (Luke 13:15). A manger held the feed.

5. Work happened in the courtyard. There the women ground grain for the day’s bread (Matthew 24:41) and baked it in a clay oven.

Why it matters. The King of kings did not grow up in a palace. He lived in a home like this one, with one lamp, a flat roof and bread baked each morning. When He taught, He talked about lamps, lost coins and houses built on rock, because He had lived among them.

Flight to Egypt

7
“When he arose, he took the young child and his mother by night, and departed into Egypt” (Matthew 2:14)
Read together: Matthew 2:13–18 · Hosea 11:1 · Jeremiah 31:15

After the Wise Men left, an angel of the Lord appeared to Joseph in a dream. “Arise, and take the young child and his mother, and flee into Egypt, and be thou there until I bring thee word: for Herod will seek the young child to destroy him.”

Joseph did not wait until morning. He got up, took Yeshua and Mary, and left that very night. This was the third time Joseph obeyed God’s message in a dream, and each time he obeyed right away.

Why Egypt?

Egypt was outside Herod’s kingdom, so Herod could not reach them there. It was also home to many Jewish families, especially in the great city of Alexandria. And God had a reason in His Word: the prophet Hosea had written, “Out of Egypt have I called my son” (Hosea 11:1). Long ago, God brought Israel out of Egypt. Now His own Son would come out of Egypt too.

Herod’s Anger

When Herod realized the Wise Men had tricked him, he became furious. He ordered his soldiers to put to death every boy in and around Bethlehem who was two years old or younger. It was a terrible and cruel act, and Matthew says it fulfilled Jeremiah’s words about Rachel weeping for her children (Jeremiah 31:15).

But Herod could not stop God’s plan. The young King was already safe in Egypt, protected by His Father in heaven.

Lesson Seven · Reading 3

Can Any Good Thing Come Out of Nazareth?

7
“And Nathanael said unto him, Can there any good thing come out of Nazareth? Philip saith unto him, Come and see” (John 1:46)
Read together: Luke 1:26 · John 1:45–46 · Luke 4:16 · Isaiah 11:1

The Old Testament names hundreds of towns in Israel. Nazareth is not one of them. No king was born there. No prophet preached there. No battle was won there. When God chose a hometown for His Son, He chose a place the Scriptures had never mentioned.

A village off the main road. Nazareth sat on a ridge in the hills of lower Galilee, about fifteen miles from the Sea of Galilee. Below it stretched the wide Jezreel Valley, where armies and traders passed. About four miles away stood Sepphoris, a busy city. Nazareth itself was only a farming village. Even its neighbors looked down on it. When Nathanael, who came from nearby Cana, heard where Yeshua was from, he asked, “Can there any good thing come out of Nazareth?”

Hands that worked. Families in Nazareth grew grain, olives and grapes on the hillsides, and the village shared a spring for its water. Joseph was a tekton, the word translated “carpenter” (Matthew 13:55). It means a builder, a man who worked with wood and stone. Yeshua learned that trade beside him, and the neighbors later called Him “the carpenter” (Mark 6:3).

A home filled with Scripture. Nazareth had a synagogue, and Luke says it was Yeshua’s custom to be there every Sabbath (Luke 4:16). In a Hebrew home, God’s words were taught to children at the table, on the road, at bedtime and at sunrise (Deuteronomy 6:6–7). The King grew up hearing the very Scriptures that spoke about Him.

Why it matters. Isaiah said the Messiah would grow up “as a root out of a dry ground” (Isaiah 53:2), with nothing impressive to draw a crowd. Nazareth was that dry ground. God “hath chosen the weak things of the world to confound the things which are mighty” (1 Corinthians 1:27). No one is from a place too small for God to use.

Teaching Nuggets

Key word

Isaiah 11:1 promises a “Branch” from Jesse’s roots. The Hebrew word is netzer. Many teachers believe the name Nazareth comes from the same word.

Prophecy and fulfillment

Isaiah 53:2 → John 1:46. The Messiah would not look important. Nathanael’s question proves the prophecy true.

Ask

Philip did not argue with Nathanael. He said, “Come and see.” Who could be invited to come and see Yeshua this week?
