A Note on Names and Sources

Yeshua and Jesus

In this book you will see both names, Yeshua and Jesus. Yeshua is the Hebrew name that Mary and Joseph gave their son. It means “God saves” or “God is salvation.” Jesus is the same name after it passed through Greek and Latin into English. Using His Hebrew name helps children remember that Jesus was born into a Hebrew family, in the land of Israel, as the promised King from David's line.

Bible Verses

The verses printed at the top of each reading are from the King James Version. Feel free to read the passages aloud from the Bible translation your class uses.

History and Tradition

Some parts of this study, such as the Tower of the Flock, the season of Yeshua's birth, and the homeland of the Wise Men, draw on Jewish history, ancient writings, and the work of Bible scholars. Where good teachers disagree, the Teaching Nuggets show the different views. The Bible itself is always our final word.

The Promised King: Son of David

1
“Behold, the days come, saith the LORD, that I will raise unto David a righteous Branch, and a King shall reign and prosper” (Jeremiah 23:5)
Read together: Matthew 1:1 · 2 Samuel 7:12–16 · Isaiah 11:1–2

Long ago, God made a promise to King David, the shepherd boy from Bethlehem who became Israel’s greatest king. God told David that one of his descendants would sit on his throne, and that this King’s kingdom would last forever (2 Samuel 7:12–16).

For hundreds of years, God’s people waited. The prophets kept the promise alive. Jeremiah said God would raise up a “righteous Branch” for David. Isaiah said a shoot would grow from the stump of Jesse, David’s father, and the Spirit of the Lord would rest on Him (Isaiah 11:1–2).

Waiting for a King

By the time Yeshua was born, Israel was ruled by Rome. Many people were looking for a warrior king who would drive out the Romans with an army. The Pharisees, who carefully kept the Torah, believed they were protecting God’s ways. But God’s King came in a way almost no one expected: as a baby, born to a humble family.

“Son of David”

Matthew begins his Gospel with these words: “Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham.” Calling Yeshua the Son of David was a bold announcement. It was a title for the Messiah, the promised King. Matthew wanted every reader to know that Yeshua was the One God had promised.

Teaching Nuggets

Ask the class

What kind of king do you think people were hoping for? How is Yeshua different?

Fulfilled prophecy

Yeshua fulfilled more than 300 Old Testament prophecies. Have children find Jeremiah 23:5–6 and Isaiah 11:1–2 in their Bibles.

Key word

Messiah (Hebrew) and Christ (Greek) both mean “Anointed One,” the chosen King.

Notes
Did You Know?
Jesse, David’s father, lived in Bethlehem. That is why Isaiah calls the coming King a shoot from “the stem of Jesse.”

Two Family Lines: The Right to Be King

1
“And there shall come forth a rod out of the stem of Jesse, and a Branch shall grow out of his roots” (Isaiah 11:1)
Read together: Matthew 1:1–17 · Luke 3:23–38

The Bible gives us two family trees for Yeshua. Matthew 1 and Luke 3 list His ancestors, but the lists are different. Why? Together they show that Yeshua had the right to be King through both sides of His family.

The Legal Right: Joseph’s Line

Matthew traces the family from Abraham to King David, and then through David’s son Solomon, the line of the kings, all the way to Joseph. Joseph was not Yeshua’s birth father, but by adopting Him, Joseph gave Yeshua the legal right to David’s throne.

The Blood Right: Mary’s Line

Luke traces the family through David’s son Nathan. Many Bible teachers believe this is Mary’s family line. Through Mary, Yeshua was truly born from David’s family. He had the blood right to the throne, just as Isaiah promised.

Adopted Into His Family

Here is good news for us: just as Yeshua was adopted into Joseph’s family, God adopts us into His family. “God sent forth his Son … that we might receive the adoption of sons” (Galatians 4:4–5). We who were far away are brought near “by the blood of Christ” (Ephesians 2:13).

Three Sets of Fourteen

Matthew arranges the names into three groups of fourteen generations (Matthew 1:17). Matthew even lists some women with surprising stories, like Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and Bathsheba, showing that God’s grace reaches everyone.

Teaching Nuggets

Legal and Blood

Legal right: Joseph, Solomon’s line (Jer. 23:5–6).

Blood right: Mary, Nathan’s line (Isa. 11).

Numbers

“14” is often linked to deliverance and salvation; “3” to divine completeness. Three sets of fourteen cover Israel’s long seasons of hardship before the Deliverer came.

Gospel link

We are adopted into God’s family (Gal. 4:4–5) and brought near by His blood (Eph. 2:13).

Notes
Did You Know?
Luke’s list goes all the way back to Adam, “which was the son of God” (Luke 3:38). There were about 2,000 years from Abraham to Yeshua.

An Angel Visits Mary

1
“Behold, a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel” (Isaiah 7:14)
Read together: Luke 1:26–38 · Matthew 1:18–25

In the hill country of Galilee sat a little village called Nazareth. In this quiet place lived a young woman named Mary, who was promised in marriage to a carpenter named Joseph.

One day God sent the angel Gabriel to Mary. “Greetings, you who are highly favored! The Lord is with you,” he said. Mary was troubled, but Gabriel told her not to be afraid. She would have a baby boy, the Son of the Most High, and she was to call Him Yeshua. “The Lord God shall give unto him the throne of his father David,” Gabriel said, “and of his kingdom there shall be no end” (Luke 1:32–33).

“How can this be?” Mary asked. Gabriel explained that the Holy Spirit would come upon her, so the child would be called the Son of God. “For with God nothing shall be impossible.” Mary answered with a trusting heart: “Behold the handmaid of the Lord; be it unto me according to thy word.”

Joseph’s Dream

When Joseph learned that Mary was expecting a baby, he was worried and did not know what to do. Then an angel spoke to him in a dream: the baby was from the Holy Spirit, and He would save His people from their sins. Joseph woke up, obeyed God, and took Mary to be his wife.

Teaching Nuggets

Before you begin

Open in prayer, asking God to prepare the children’s hearts to be open and ready to receive understanding of today’s lesson.

“Son of David”

Gabriel’s words point straight back to God’s promise to David. Ask: which promise from Reading 1 is coming true?

Mary’s faith

Mary did not understand everything, but she trusted God. Ask: When is it hard to trust God?

Notes

This would not be the only time God would speak to Joseph through a dream! God spoke to him in four dreams in all (Matthew 1:20; 2:13; 2:19–20; 2:22).
The Hebrew word for salvation is ישועה (yeshu’ah), which means deliverance or rescue. It comes from the root ישע (Y-Sh-A), meaning to save or rescue. Listen closely: it sounds just like His name, Yeshua!

His Name Is Yeshua

1
“And she shall bring forth a son, and thou shalt call his name JESUS: for he shall save his people from their sins” (Matthew 1:21)
Read together: Matthew 1:20–25 · Isaiah 49:6

In Bible times, a name was more than a label. It often told something about who a person was or what God would do through them. So when the angel told Joseph what to name the baby, the name carried a message.

To His family and friends, the baby was called Yehoshua, or in its shorter form, Yeshua. The name means “God is salvation” or “God saves.” Every time someone called His name, they were saying what He came to do: “He shall save his people from their sins.”

A Hebrew Name

Mary and Joseph were Hebrews, so it was natural for them to give their son a Hebrew name. Later, when the good news spread to people who spoke Greek, His name was written Iēsous. From there it came into Latin and, many centuries later, into English as Jesus. In fact, the letter “J” was not used in English until about 500 years ago!

Immanuel: God With Us

Matthew tells us that all this happened to fulfill Isaiah’s prophecy: the virgin would have a son, and they would call Him Immanuel, which means “God with us” (Matthew 1:23). Yeshua is both. He is the God who saves, and the God who came to be with us.

Teaching Nuggets

Name study

Yeshua is the same name as Joshua in the Old Testament. Joshua led Israel into the Promised Land; Yeshua leads us into God’s kingdom.

Joseph’s obedience

Joseph “did as the angel of the Lord had bidden him” (Matt. 1:24). Ask: How did Joseph show faith?

Activity tip

Hebrew is read from right to left. Show the class before they try the writing page.

Notes
Did You Know?
The name Yeshua appears in Hebrew as ישוע. It is made from four Hebrew letters: Yod, Shin, Vav, and Ayin.

Life in Nazareth

1
“And he came and dwelt in a city called Nazareth: that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophets, He shall be called a Nazarene” (Matthew 2:23)
Read together: Luke 1:26 · Luke 2:39–40 · John 1:46

When Mary and Joseph were growing up, Nazareth was a small farming village in the hills of Galilee. Fewer than 200 people lived there. It was so small and unimportant that one man later asked, “Can there any good thing come out of Nazareth?” (John 1:46).

Homes and Gardens

Most families in Nazareth had a strip of land where they grew vegetables. Their homes were built of mud brick or stone. A house usually had two or three rooms and an open courtyard. Life centered on the home. That is where families talked about the news, their faith, and the events of the day, often while they shared a meal.

Mealtimes

Hebrew families usually ate two meals a day. The first was in the late morning or around midday, and the second was in the evening at home. Meals were simple: bread, lentil stew, fruit, and eggs. Families also enjoyed fish, goat meat, cheese, olives, and wine. Red meat was saved for special occasions. Instead of sitting on chairs at a table, people often reclined on cushions on the floor to eat.

God Chose a Humble Place

God could have chosen a palace in Jerusalem. Instead, He chose a young woman from a tiny village and a carpenter who worked with his hands. God loves to do great things through humble people.

Teaching Nuggets

Compare and share

Ask children to compare a day in Nazareth with their own day: food, homes, chores.

“Nazarene”

The name Nazareth may come from the Hebrew word netzer, “branch.” It connects to Isaiah 11:1, the Branch from Jesse!

Galilee

Nazareth was about 65 miles north of Jerusalem.

Notes
Did You Know?
Archaeologists have found the remains of simple houses, olive presses, and stone water cisterns in Nazareth from the time of Yeshua.

A Hebrew Wedding

1
“Then Joseph being raised from sleep did as the angel of the Lord had bidden him, and took unto him his wife” (Matthew 1:24)
Read together: Matthew 1:18–25 · Luke 1:27

Weddings in ancient Israel were very different from weddings today. A Hebrew wedding happened in two big steps, often about a year apart.

Step One: The Betrothal

In Hebrew tradition, the father of the groom often chose a bride for his son. The groom’s family and the bride’s father agreed on a bride price and signed a marriage contract called a ketubah. Once the ketubah was signed, the couple was legally husband and wife, but they did not live together yet. If they wanted to separate, they needed a divorce, and the groom would lose the price he had paid.

Step Two: The Wedding Feast

During the waiting time, the groom prepared a home for his bride, often a room added onto his father’s house. When the groom’s father saw that everything was ready, he told his son to go and get his bride! The groom brought her home for a wedding feast that could last up to seven days. This final step was called the nisuin.

Mary and Joseph

Mary and Joseph were in the first step, betrothed but not yet living together, when Mary was found to be expecting a baby. That is why Joseph was so troubled. After the angel explained God’s plan in a dream, Joseph took Mary to be his wife.

Teaching Nuggets

A picture of Yeshua

Yeshua used wedding pictures when He spoke of His return: “I go to prepare a place for you” (John 14:2–3). Like a groom, He is preparing a home for His people.

Key words

Ketubah: marriage contract.

Nisuin: the final wedding step when the bride is brought home.

Discuss

Why was Joseph’s choice to obey God brave?

Notes
Did You Know?
Joseph planned to end the betrothal quietly so Mary would not be shamed (Matthew 1:19). The Bible calls him “a just man.”

The Census

1
“And it came to pass in those days, that there went out a decree from Caesar Augustus, that all the world should be taxed” (Luke 2:1)
Read together: Luke 2:1–5 · Micah 5:2

Far away in the city of Rome, the emperor Caesar Augustus made a law. Everyone in the Roman Empire had to be counted in a census and registered for taxes. Each family had to go to its own hometown to sign up.

Joseph belonged to the family of King David, and David’s hometown was Bethlehem in Judea. So Joseph had to leave Nazareth and travel south to Bethlehem, and he took Mary with him, even though her baby would soon be born.

A Long Journey

The trip from Nazareth to Bethlehem was about 90 miles. Traveling on foot, perhaps with a donkey to carry supplies, it would have taken about a week. Many travelers followed the road along the Jordan River valley and then climbed up through the hills past Jerusalem.

God Was in Charge

Caesar Augustus thought he was in control of the whole world. But God was using even an emperor’s law to keep His promise! Seven hundred years earlier, the prophet Micah had written that the Ruler of Israel would be born in Bethlehem (Micah 5:2). The census brought Mary and Joseph to exactly the right town at exactly the right time.

As the fall season was about to begin, Mary and Joseph set out on the road to Bethlehem.

Teaching Nuggets

God’s timing

Galatians 4:4 says God sent His Son “when the fulness of the time was come.” Ask: How did God use the census?

Rulers

Caesar Augustus ruled Rome from 27 BC to AD 14. Quirinius governed Syria (Luke 2:2).

Map it

Trace the route from Nazareth to Bethlehem on a map of Israel.

Notes
Did You Know?
Bethlehem means “House of Bread.” The One who called Himself “the bread of life” (John 6:35) was born in the House of Bread!

Migdal Eder: The Tower of the Flock

2
“And thou, O tower of the flock, the strong hold of the daughter of Zion, unto thee shall it come, even the first dominion” (Micah 4:8)
Read together: Genesis 35:19–21 · Micah 4:8 · Micah 5:2 · Luke 2:8–12

Right at the edge of Bethlehem, in the fields just outside the town, stood a stone watchtower called Migdal Eder. In Hebrew, Migdal Eder means “Tower of the Flock.” It was so close to Bethlehem that the shepherds who worked there could walk into town in a few minutes.

Where Rachel Was Buried

The Tower of the Flock first appears in the story of Jacob. When Jacob’s wife Rachel died, she “was buried in the way to Ephrath, which is Bethlehem,” and Jacob set up a pillar on her grave (Genesis 35:19–20). The very next verse says Jacob “spread his tent beyond the tower of Edar” (Genesis 35:21). Edar is the same Hebrew word as Eder. So from Israel’s earliest days, the Tower of the Flock stood right beside Bethlehem.

A Prophecy the Shepherds Knew

Hundreds of years later, the prophet Micah spoke to the Tower of the Flock: “Unto thee shall it come, even the first dominion” (Micah 4:8). A few verses later he announced that the Ruler of Israel would come from Bethlehem (Micah 5:2). The shepherds of Migdal Eder knew these prophecies about their own tower. So when the angel spoke, they knew exactly where to look for the Messiah: right there in Bethlehem, near the Tower of the Flock.

Shepherd Language

The angel said, “Ye shall find the babe wrapped in swaddling clothes, lying in a manger” (Luke 2:12). That was shepherd language! These were the very words these shepherds used every lambing season. God gave them a sign that only shepherds like them would fully understand.

What Was the Tower Like?

Watchtowers like this were usually two stories tall. From the top, shepherds watched for wolves and thieves. The lower level was a sheltered place where ewes gave birth to their lambs.

Teaching Nuggets

Ancient sources

The Mishnah (Shekalim 7:4) describes animals found in the area near Jerusalem as fit for temple offerings. The Targum on Genesis 35:21 calls Migdal Eder the place where “King Messiah will be revealed in the end of days.”

2

The Bible does not give us the date of Yeshua’s birth. But it gives us clues, and Bible students have studied them for centuries. There are two main views.

Clues for a Fall Birth

Shepherds in the fields. In Judea, shepherds stayed out in the fields with their flocks at night from spring until early fall. The weather was mild enough to sleep outdoors.

Zechariah’s temple service. Luke tells us Zechariah served in the priestly “course of Abijah” (Luke 1:5). Using the Hebrew calendar, his service falls in midsummer, John the Baptist’s birth near Passover, and Yeshua’s birth six months later, in the early fall.

The feasts. The fall feasts, Trumpets, the Day of Atonement, and Tabernacles (Sukkot), brought crowds of travelers. That would explain why there was no room! A Roman census would also make sense in a season when people were already traveling.

Clues for a Spring Birth

Some Bible scholars count Zechariah’s service differently, placing John’s birth in March and Yeshua’s around March or April.

Spring is lambing season, when shepherds kept watch day and night.

Some early Christian writers placed the birth in March or April.

What Matters Most

Both views are possible, but calculations that use the Hebrew calendar point most strongly to the fall. What matters most is not the date, but that God kept His promise right on time.

Teaching Nuggets

Hebrew calendar

The Hebrew calendar follows the moon and adds leap months. The feasts fall on fixed Hebrew dates. Using the modern calendar can shift the calculations.

Tabernacles

John 1:14 says the Word “dwelt” among us. The Greek word means “tabernacled”!

Keep it friendly

Let children hear both views. Christians who love the Bible hold either one.

3
“Behold, there came wise men from the east to Jerusalem, saying, Where is he that is born King of the Jews? for we have seen his star in the east” (Matthew 2:1–2)
Read together: Matthew 2:1–2 · Daniel 2:48 · Numbers 24:17

Many songs call them “three kings,” but the Bible never says they were kings. Matthew calls them magoi, or Magi, a well-known group of priest-scholars from the lands east of Israel.

Priests, Scholars, and King-Makers

The Magi first came from the Medes and Persians. They were experts in the stars, astrologers, and students of dreams, ancient writings, and the business of kings. At the time Yeshua was born, Persia was part of the Parthian Empire, the great rival of Rome. In Parthia, Magi sat on the king’s council. Together with the nobles, they had so much power that they could help choose a new king, or remove one. People called them king-makers.

Why Would They Care About a Jewish King?

Hundreds of years earlier, the Jews were carried away to Babylon. One of them, Daniel, became “chief of the governors over all the wise men of Babylon” (Daniel 2:48). Daniel’s writings included a timetable for the coming Messiah (Daniel 9:24–27).

Many Jewish families stayed in Babylon and Persia for centuries. They knew the prophecy of Balaam: “There shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel” (Numbers 24:17). For scholars who studied the sky, a prophecy about a star would catch their attention! Generations of Eastern scholars may have kept and passed on this knowledge.

Teaching Nuggets

Key word

Magi (Greek magoi) is where we get the word “magic,” but these men were not tricksters. They were respected priests and scholars.

Astronomy is studying the sun, moon, stars, and planets as God made them. Astrology is the belief that the stars control or foretell people’s lives, which the Bible warns against (Deuteronomy 18:10–12). The Magi practiced both, yet God used the sky they studied to lead them to Yeshua.

Daniel’s legacy

Daniel’s influence is a big reason many scholars believe Eastern Magi were expecting a Jewish King.

It was probably not a comet, because no recorded comet matches the time. And it was not an ordinary star sitting still above one house. The Parthian Empire

3
“When Herod the king had heard these things, he was troubled, and all Jerusalem with him” (Matthew 2:3)
Read together: Matthew 2:1–3

Parthia was a vast empire that stretched across the lands we now call Iran and Iraq, southeast of the Caspian Sea. The Parthians ruled the East at the same time the Romans ruled the lands around the Mediterranean Sea.

Rome’s Great Rival

Rome started many wars with Parthia, but it never conquered it. In fact, the Parthians defeated Roman armies many times. In 53 BC they crushed a Roman army at the Battle of Carrhae. And from 40 to 37 BC, Parthian forces even took control of Judea and Jerusalem for a short time! After that, Rome wanted peace with Parthia, and Roman officials knew that starting another war could cost them their jobs, or their lives.

The Magi’s Journey

The Magi likely set out from the Parthian capital, Seleucia–Ctesiphon, on the Tigris River. They would have followed the trade routes of the Silk Road along the Euphrates River, turned south through Damascus, and come down to Jerusalem. The trip was about 700 to 900 miles and could take two to four months.

So imagine the scene: a large group of powerful officials from Rome’s greatest enemy rides into Jerusalem asking, “Where is he that is born King of the Jews?” No wonder Herod was troubled!

Who Was King Herod?

3

They Bowed Down and Worshiped Him

4
“And when they were come into the house, they saw the young child with Mary his mother, and fell down, and worshipped him” (Matthew 2:11)

The Wise Men left Herod’s palace and took the short road south to Bethlehem. Then they saw the star again, going ahead of them, until it stopped over the place where the young child was. “They rejoiced with exceeding great joy!”

Kings Bow Before the King

Remember who these men were: powerful officials from the Parthian Empire, rich and important king-makers who could help put a ruler on the throne. Yet when they came into the simple house and saw the young child with His mother Mary, they fell down and worshiped Him. They knew they were in the presence of a King far greater than any king on earth.

Treasures for a King

Then they opened their treasures. Eastern visitors brought rich gifts to great rulers: gold, fine cloth, carved vessels, rare spices. The Bible names three of their gifts: gold, frankincense, and myrrh. These gifts were worth a great fortune, and they likely paid for the family’s sudden journey to Egypt and their life there.

Home Another Way

That night, God warned the Wise Men in a dream not to go back to Herod. So they obeyed God and “departed into their own country another way.” Herod’s plan had failed, and the young King was safe in God’s hands.

Notice

The Wise Men obeyed God rather than the king. Ask: Why was that brave?

The Gifts: Gold, Frankincense, and Myrrh

4
“And when they had opened their treasures, they presented unto him gifts; gold, and frankincense, and myrrh” (Matthew 2:11)
Read together: Matthew 2:11 · Exodus 30:34–38 · Hebrews 4:14–16

When you look deeply into the gifts mentioned in Matthew 2, you quickly realize they were not simply offerings of honor, but a prophetic proclamation. Gold. Frankincense. Myrrh.

Matthew tells us that the Magi fell down and worshiped Him, and then, opening their treasures, they offered Him gifts. Each gift announced something essential about who Yeshua is and what His first coming would accomplish.

Gold: The King

Gold has always spoken the language of royalty. Throughout Scripture it is linked with kingship, authority, and enduring worth (1 Kings 10:18–25; Psalm 21:3). By offering gold, the Magi proclaimed that Yeshua was born a King. Not one who would seize power later, but one who held authority from the very beginning. Even before Yeshua preached a sermon or performed a miracle, the gold declared what Pilate would later question and the cross would surprisingly affirm: “Jesus of Nazareth the King of the Jews” (John 19:19).

Frankincense: The High Priest

Frankincense belonged to worship. In Israel it was reserved for sacred use, burned on the altar and mixed into the holy incense as an offering pleasing to the Lord (Exodus 30:34–38; Leviticus 2:1–2). The psalmist describes prayer rising to God “as incense” (Psalm 141:2), and Revelation links incense with the prayers of the saints before God’s throne (Revelation 5:8).

By offering frankincense, the Magi acknowledged that Yeshua was not only royal but holy, the One through whom true worship would be made possible (John 4:21–24). Frankincense quietly announces that He would be our High Priest, the One who stands between God and people (Hebrews 4:14–16).

Teaching Nuggets

Show and tell

If you can, let children smell frankincense. It is a hardened tree sap that smells sweet when burned.

Practical value

These were standard royal gifts from the East. They likely funded the family’s escape to Egypt.

Notes
Did You Know?
Frankincense and myrrh both come from the sap of small, thorny trees that grow in Arabia and East Africa.

4

Read together: Mark 15:23 · John 19:39 · Isaiah 53:3–5 · Mark 10:45

Myrrh: The Suffering Savior

Myrrh brings a quiet, serious note into the story. It was used as a medicine (Mark 15:23), but also, importantly, for burial. After Yeshua’s crucifixion, Nicodemus brought “a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about an hundred pound weight” to prepare His body for burial (John 19:39).

The presence of myrrh at Yeshua’s birth points ahead to His death. It reminds us that His mission included suffering from the very beginning. Isaiah spoke of a Servant who would be “wounded for our transgressions” (Isaiah 53:5), and Yeshua said He came “to give his life a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45). Myrrh stands as a quiet witness that the cradle was always moving toward the cross.

A Complete Testimony

Together, the gifts tell the whole story. Gold proclaims Him King. Frankincense proclaims Him Priest. Myrrh proclaims Him the Suffering Savior. The Magi did not preach sermons. They offered gifts, and those gifts spoke clearly.

What Gift Can I Bring?

This leads me to a deeply personal question. If His first coming was marked by gifts that proclaimed who He is, what about His second coming? What gift can I offer Him? Will He be delighted if the gift I bring is how I lived the life He gave me: how I served, how I obeyed, how I submitted to His Word?

Perhaps the most meaningful offering we can bring is not something placed before Him, but a life faithfully lived for Him.

Lesson Four · Reading 3

The Temple in Jerusalem

4
“And when the days of her purification according to the law of Moses were accomplished, they brought him to Jerusalem, to present him to the Lord” (Luke 2:22)
Read together: Luke 2:22–24 · 1 Kings 6 · Ezra 3

The temple in Jerusalem was the center of Hebrew life in Bible times. It was the place where God’s people came to worship, pray, and bring their offerings.

Three Temples in One Place

King Solomon, David’s son, built the first temple in the tenth century BC to house the Ark of the Covenant. Hundreds of years later, the Babylonians destroyed it, stole its treasures, and burned what was left. When the Jews returned from exile, they built a second temple in the days of Zerubbabel and Ezra, and Nehemiah rebuilt the city walls.

Herod’s Great Project

King Herod made the second temple much larger and more beautiful. One reason was to hold the huge crowds of pilgrims who came to Jerusalem for the three pilgrimage feasts: Passover, Shavuot (Pentecost), and Sukkot (Tabernacles). The historian Josephus says 10,000 workers labored on the project, and building the enormous retaining walls took years. The finished platform was as big as about two dozen football fields!

People could enter the temple courtyards, but only priests went inside the temple building itself. The temple was also the meeting place of the Sanhedrin, the highest Jewish court in the days of Roman rule. The Romans destroyed this temple in AD 70.

When the time came for the purification required by the Torah, Joseph and Mary brought baby Yeshua here to present Him to God.

Presented at the Temple

4
“For mine eyes have seen thy salvation, which thou hast prepared before the face of all people” (Luke 2:30–31)
Read together: Luke 2:21–38 · Exodus 13:2, 12 · Romans 12:1

When Yeshua was eight days old, He was circumcised and officially given the name Yehoshua, or Yeshua. About forty days after His birth, Mary and Joseph took Him to the temple, obeying the Law of Moses. Every firstborn son belonged to the Lord (Exodus 13:2, 12), and Mary brought an offering for her purification: “a pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons,” the gift of a family that could not afford a lamb (Leviticus 12:8).

To “Present” Him

Luke says they came “to present him to the Lord.” To present means to fully hand over, to offer as a gift to God. Paul uses the same word in Romans 12:1: “present your bodies a living sacrifice.” Just as Yeshua was presented to God, we can dedicate ourselves to Him, once and for all, forever!

Simeon

Simeon was a righteous and devout man in Jerusalem, known for his deep knowledge of God and his gift of understanding the times. He was waiting for “the consolation of Israel.” Consolation means comfort, encouragement, and help. The Holy Spirit had promised Simeon he would see the Messiah before he died. When he took baby Yeshua in his arms, he blessed God: “Mine eyes have seen thy salvation!” Simeon said, “Behold,” a word full of wonder and amazement.

Anna

Anna was a prophetess from the tribe of Asher. She was 84 years old, a widow who never left the temple grounds, worshiping God with fasting and prayer night and day. She gave thanks and told everyone who was waiting for redemption about the child.

Teaching Nuggets

Word study

Present (Luke 2:22; Rom. 12:1): to hand over completely as an offering to God.

Behold: shock, amazement, wonder.

Consolation: comfort, encouragement, support.

Humble offering

Two birds were the offering of the poor. The King of kings grew up in a humble family.

Apply

Romans 12:1: what does it look like to give ourselves to God?

Simeon’s name comes from the Hebrew word shama, “to hear.” Simeon listened to the Holy Spirit and was in the right place at the right time.

An Israelite House

5
“And when they were come into the house, they saw the young child with Mary his mother” (Matthew 2:11)
Read together: Matthew 2:11 · Luke 2:7 · Deuteronomy 22:8

When the Wise Men arrived in Bethlehem, Matthew says they came “into the house.” By then Yeshua was no longer a newborn in a manger. He was a young child living with His family in a home. What might that home have looked like?

Small and Simple

At the time of Yeshua, most Israelite homes were small and plain. Walls were built of mud brick or stone. The roof was flat, made of wooden beams covered with branches or straw and then packed with clay. Families climbed up to the roof to dry fruit, sleep on hot nights, or pray. God’s law even said a roof needed a short wall around it, so no one would fall (Deuteronomy 22:8)!

Rooms for Everyone

Upstairs living space: a room for sleeping and for guests.

Kitchen: often with a clay oven for baking bread.

Stable area: at night, the family’s animals came inside to stay safe from wild animals and robbers. Stone mangers held their food.

Courtyard: an open space for cooking, working, and visiting with neighbors.

Was This Yeshua’s Home?

The Bible does not describe the house where the Magi found Yeshua. But it was probably very much like this: a simple home in Bethlehem, where the King of kings lived as a little child with Mary and Joseph.

Flight to Egypt

5
“When he arose, he took the young child and his mother by night, and departed into Egypt” (Matthew 2:14)
Read together: Matthew 2:13–18 · Hosea 11:1 · Jeremiah 31:15

After the Wise Men left, an angel of the Lord appeared to Joseph in a dream. “Arise, and take the young child and his mother, and flee into Egypt, and be thou there until I bring thee word: for Herod will seek the young child to destroy him.”

Joseph did not wait until morning. He got up, took Yeshua and Mary, and left that very night. This was the third time Joseph obeyed God’s message in a dream, and each time he obeyed right away.

Why Egypt?

Egypt was outside Herod’s kingdom, so Herod could not reach them there. It was also home to many Jewish families, especially in the great city of Alexandria. And God had a reason in His Word: the prophet Hosea had written, “Out of Egypt have I called my son” (Hosea 11:1). Long ago, God brought Israel out of Egypt. Now His own Son would come out of Egypt too.

Herod’s Anger

When Herod realized the Wise Men had tricked him, he became furious. He ordered his soldiers to kill all the boys in Bethlehem and the nearby area who were two years old and under. It was a terrible and cruel act, and Matthew says it fulfilled Jeremiah’s words about Rachel weeping for her children (Jeremiah 31:15).

But Herod could not stop God’s plan. The young King was already safe in Egypt, protected by His Father in heaven.

Teaching Nuggets

Handle with care

Be gentle when sharing Herod’s cruelty with young children. Focus on God’s protection and Joseph’s obedience.

Provision

The gifts of the Magi likely paid for the trip and for living in Egypt. God provided before they even knew they needed it!

Distance

From Bethlehem to the Nile delta was about 250 to 300 miles, a journey of two weeks or more.

5

Read together: Matthew 2:19–23 · Luke 2:39–40

Mary, Joseph, and young Yeshua stayed in Egypt until Herod died. Then, once again, an angel of the Lord appeared to Joseph in a dream: “Arise, and take the young child and his mother, and go into the land of Israel: for they are dead which sought the young child’s life.”

Joseph got up and brought his family back. But when he heard that Herod’s son Archelaus was ruling Judea, he was afraid to go there. God warned him in another dream, so Joseph took the family north to Galilee, to their old hometown of Nazareth.

The Branch Grows Up

Remember Isaiah’s promise of a Branch growing from the stump of Jesse? The name Nazareth may come from the Hebrew word netzer, “branch.” The promised Branch from David’s family grew up in the “Branch Town”! Luke tells us “the child grew, and waxed strong in spirit, filled with wisdom: and the grace of God was upon him” (Luke 2:40).

The King Has Come

Look back at the whole story. God promised a King from David’s line. He sent Gabriel to Mary and an angel to Joseph. He used an emperor’s census to bring them to Bethlehem. He revealed the Lamb of God to shepherds at the Tower of the Flock, and led Wise Men from Parthia by a star. And He protected His Son from a jealous king.

Every promise came true. The King has come, and He invites each of us to follow Him.
